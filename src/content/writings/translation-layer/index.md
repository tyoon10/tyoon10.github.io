The hardest marketing problem in AI is not getting attention. It is being believed by people who can check your work. Developers and technical buyers reject anything that is not true faster than any other audience, and they are the audience for every product that matters right now. So the job is not to make the technology sound impressive. It is to close the gap between what the technology actually does and what a person understands they can now do with it, in a sentence that survives an engineer reading it. That seam has a name in my head. I call it the **translation layer**, and it is where I have spent my career.

Standing there takes two fluencies that rarely live in one person. You have to read the system well enough to know what actually changed, and write it well enough that a busy human acts on it. Most people have one. Marketers who write the clean sentence wait for a brief because they cannot read the roadmap. Engineers who can read the roadmap lose the value in translation because they cannot write the sentence. **I did not come at this from marketing. I came at it from building.** I co-founded and sold an AI insurance company where I owned the product and the machine learning, and I built financial data platforms at scale as a technical product manager. What I kept noticing was that the highest-leverage thing I did was never the model. It was the sentence that made the model make sense to the person who had to decide.

The clearest example of what I mean is a piece I built on [the inference stack](/writings/agents-need-better-infrastructure/). I took the layer underneath the model, quantization, inference engines, model routing, and showed with real telemetry how those low-level choices change what a developer can actually build at the application layer. It is marketing, in that it makes a capability legible and worth wanting. It survives engineering review, in that every claim is measured, not asserted. Legible and true at the same time is the whole game for a technical audience, and most content picks one.

Positioning, underneath the craft, is just choosing the one true sentence to lead with. I have done it under real stakes. At the insurance company I positioned a policy like an investment portfolio for first-time buyers, which turned a grudge purchase into a decision they wanted to make. I have argued in public that [context engineering, not prompt engineering, is the skill that separates AI-native product managers from everyone else](/writings/ai-superpower-for-pm/), which reframed a category for a lot of readers. And I have spent a year making frontier AI land for the people who have to adopt it, from first-time prompters to CTOs, and grown a developer community from zero to over 1,300. Holding one message steady across every one of those rooms is not a soft skill. It is the architecture.

I also build the tools I market with. I run my own positioning out of what I think of as a **constitution**: a single governed corpus of proof points, personas, narrative, and voice that every draft pulls from, so the story does not drift across channels or people. I test whether a message lands the way I test code, with evals, because that is a measurable question and not a matter of taste. My public repositories, [OptiMind](https://github.com/tyoon10/optimind) and [claude-ensemble](https://github.com/tyoon10/claude-ensemble), are these systems running and operated daily, not slideware.

None of this started as a career plan. I built things, sold them, and taught them, and the part I was best at was always the same: turning a technical capability into a sentence a specific person acts on. The technology is only going to get more powerful and less legible from here, which means someone has to stand at the seam and keep it true and clear at the same time. I am already doing that work. I would like to do it where it matters most.
