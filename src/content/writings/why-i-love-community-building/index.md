It was past midnight at a 36-hour hackathon in Manhattan, and a team waved me over to show me their demo. The build was genuinely impressive: clean interface, fast inference, a model running on hardware in the room. I watched it run, then asked the question I ask most: *"What user problem does this solve? Does this add value to how users currently get the job done?"*

They went quiet. One of them started to answer, stopped, and looked back at the screen. Twenty minutes later they came back, not with more features, but with a sharper version of the problem they were actually solving. The demo had not changed much. The team had.

That moment is why I do this. I was a judge and a mentor that weekend, and the part that stayed with me was not the scoring. It was watching a few honest questions turn a clever build into a real one. I have done this work at three different scales, and each one taught me something the last could not.

Here are three reasons I love community building.

## Reason 1: Getting people into the room

On Valentine's Day morning, the Geffen Hall Board Room at Columbia Business School was packed by 8:30 AM. Nearly 300 people, most of them strangers to each other, had shown up to build for twelve hours. I helped organize it. The registration line wrapped through the hallway before the doors even opened.

The numbers:

- **~300** builders in the room
- **60+** teams that submitted final projects
- **$10,000+** in prizes
- **8** corporate sponsors
- **12** hours from kickoff to final pitch

But the numbers only capture part of it. The real bet was about *who* we let in. Most campus hackathons draw from a single school. We opened the doors to everyone: business and engineering students, startup founders and local business owners, seasoned developers and first-time vibe coders, all in one room. An MBA with a fintech idea sat down next to an engineer who could build it in hours. That pairing does not happen by accident. Someone has to convene it.

By the close, the room emptied with the particular energy of people who shipped something real next to strangers who had become collaborators. **The room is where everything else becomes possible**, and someone has to open the door.

## Reason 2: Making builders better once they are in the room

Getting people in the room is the start. The harder, more rewarding work is what happens once they are building.

Two weeks after Columbia, I was a judge and mentor at Mistral AI's first worldwide hackathon, the New York edition of a 36-hour sprint that ran across seven cities at once. Over 7,000 people applied, 1,000 were selected, and the prize pool topped $200K. My actual job was small and specific: sit with teams, look at what they built, and ask the questions that move them forward.

The mentoring conversations followed a pattern. A team would show me a technically impressive demo, and I would push on two things. The first was the problem: *"What user problem does this solve? Does this add value to how users currently get the job done?"* The second was responsibility: *"How do you ensure safety? Have you been considerate about values including privacy, public good, and inclusion?"*

These were not rhetorical. The teams that paused, rethought, and came back with sharper framing were the ones that outperformed. I saw builds that doubled down on technical sophistication alone, and they blew me away, but none of them made it to the top. **The work that wins is the work that solves a real problem and is deployed responsibly.** Pure cleverness never carried a team across the line.

A good question, asked at the right moment, multiplies what a builder can do for the rest of the night and long after the event ends. That multiplier is what I want to spend my time on.

## Reason 3: Building the scaffolding so it lasts

A great moment is not the same as a lasting community. The third reason I love this work is the least visible one: building the machinery that makes the moment repeatable.

The 300-person event did not run on enthusiasm. Our co-organizing team grew to 20+ people, and every person owned a lane: venue approval, sponsor onboarding and API access distribution, registration flow, A/V, catering windows, judging coordination. When 300 people are building under a clock, there is no margin for ambiguity in operations. I helped run that machine. The clarity of who owns what is the actual product.

Scaffolding is also what you build between events, and between organizers. I co-organize campus AI community events across New York: a Claude Builder Club kickoff, a Claude Builder hackathon, a GDG Build-with-AI hackathon, an AI for Greater Good NYC event, a Claude for Product Management workshop, a Building the Future of Finance with Claude talk. But building Claude's NYC builder ecosystem from zero to over 1,300 developers, leading a business-school AI club and its startup challenge, and founding BRAIN NYC taught me where the real leverage is. It is not running one more event myself. It is developing the other organizers: thinking past one day's turnout to what sustained engagement looks like across a semester, and connecting the campus leads so they trade what works and inherit the sponsor-onboarding flow, the judging rubric, and the run-of-show instead of each solving the same problem alone.

Some of that scaffolding is not a metaphor. It is code. My instinct with repetitive work is the same in operations as in engineering: automate it, and ship the working version tonight rather than the perfect one next week. I build and ship tooling on GitHub, I shipped agentic workflows as a Manus AI Fellow, and I write technical deep-dives on agent infrastructure, file-based memory for coding agents, and RAG. Before any of this, I co-founded Concat, an AI-native insurance-tech company that a major insurer acquired, where I led the in-house NLP and machine-learning work: three government AI research grants, a top-20 global Kaggle finish, and models shipped into a product people used through authenticated insurer APIs. I have built under real deadlines, not only organized rooms where other people build.

My own trajectory is a small proof that this scaffolding compounds: from organizing club-level events, to city-scale hackathons, to judging a global competition backed by some of the most significant companies in AI. The connective thread was not credentials. It was showing up and doing the work nobody sees, some of it operational, some of it code. **Communities that last are built, not gathered.**

The questions, the tooling, and the open door are what a community keeps after the room clears. The student leaders and developers who fill these rooms are already shaping how the next generation builds with AI, and that is what I want to keep building for.
